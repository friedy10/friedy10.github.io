2025 — now Northwestern finding

Side channels on quantum decoders

Fault-tolerant quantum computing depends on a classical decoder that interprets error syndromes in real time. I showed, for the first time on real hardware, that this decoder's execution time and power draw leak the syndrome it is processing — the error-correction machinery betrays the errors it exists to hide.

To measure it I ported the distributed union-find decoder (Helios) and a constant-latency lookup-table decoder to a Xilinx Artix-7 and built the full measurement pipeline, capturing per-decode timing and power across hundreds of thousands of decodes of real Google Willow surface-code syndromes at distances 3 and 5. From timing alone the traces fingerprint individual IBM Heron processors with up to 89% accuracy, and recover code distance, logical error rate, and detector-firing structure.

CAPIO — giving applications the device, not the keys to it

Kernel bypass lets an application talk straight to a NIC or NVMe drive, which is fast, but the registers it legitimately needs often share a page with registers it absolutely should not touch. CAPIO is a capability-based design that grants userspace exactly the MMIO registers it is entitled to and isolates the privileged ones at the same granularity, on commodity hardware.

I built the kernel and device-driver mechanisms underneath it, and mentor the students working on the systems implementation.

2024 — now Northwestern system

TrustCheckpoints — a root of trust with no secret to steal

Attestation normally rests on a key burned into the device or a dedicated trusted element, both of which are things an attacker can extract or a manufacturer can get wrong. TrustCheckpoints needs neither: a verifier issues randomized polynomial memory challenges whose timing a compromised device cannot fake, so malware gives itself away by being too slow.

The prototype is end to end — firmware, hypervisor, Linux, and an external verifier — running on ARM Cortex-A53.

2022 — 2025 Pitt · Northwestern · Michigan system

Privacy Backplane — inverting DRM so people set the policy

Ubiquitous sensing is already a fact of life: large fleets of IoT devices quietly record and analyse personal information about whoever walks past them. Blanket legal frameworks like GDPR and CCPA answer this with one policy for everyone. The Privacy Backplane is a legal-technical framework for the opposite — environments must negotiate a clear policy with each individual covering what may be collected about them and how it is used, enforce it, and then certify that they did.

It is classic DRM with the roles reversed. The individual is the content producer, and holds both the knowledge of and the control over the policies attached to their data. I built systems mechanisms underneath it, including an ARM/Linux video pipeline and the kernel-level components that enforce policy on the device itself.
